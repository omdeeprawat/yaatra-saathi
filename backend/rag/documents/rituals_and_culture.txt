NANDA DEVI RAJ JAT YATRA — RITUALS, CULTURE AND MYTHOLOGY

The Mythology of Nanda Devi
Goddess Nanda Devi is considered the patron goddess of Uttarakhand and is worshipped across Kumaon and Garhwal. She is believed to be a form of Goddess Parvati, the divine consort of Lord Shiva. According to legend, Nanda was the daughter of a mountain chieftain and married Lord Shiva. The Raj Jat Yatra symbolises her journey from her parental home (the mountains of Uttarakhand) to her marital home at Mount Kailash.

The Raj Jat — A Royal Procession
The word Raj Jat means Royal Procession. Historically, the kings of the erstwhile Garhwal and Kumaon kingdoms led the procession. Today the tradition is maintained by the descendants of these royal families who carry the royal palanquin (palki) of Goddess Nanda.

Opening Rituals at Nauti
The Yatra formally begins with elaborate rituals at Nauti. The Goddess is invoked, the Chausingha ram is blessed, and the royal palki is brought out in procession. Local priests perform havan (fire ceremonies) and the mountain air fills with the sound of traditional instruments including dhols (drums), turhi (a long brass horn), and ransingha (a curved horn instrument).

The Nanda Devi Jagar
The Jagar is a sacred oral tradition unique to Kumaon and Garhwal. During the Yatra, Jagaris (traditional bards) perform all-night singing sessions recounting the mythology of Goddess Nanda. These performances are accompanied by a large metal plate (thali) used as a percussion instrument. The Jagar is considered a form of worship and a way to invoke the Goddess.

Doli and Palki
The Goddess travels in a doli (a covered palanquin carried on the shoulders of priests). The doli is elaborately decorated with flowers, gold ornaments, and sacred cloth. Devotees consider it extremely auspicious to carry the doli even for a few steps.

Village Rituals Along the Route
Every village along the route has its own traditions for welcoming the procession. Villagers line the route with flowers, lamps, and offerings. Local deities (called devtas) from surrounding villages join the procession at specific points, their own dolis and symbols carried by their own priest communities.

The Significance of Wan Village
Wan is the most important halt of the entire Yatra. The procession stays here for two nights. Grand rituals, community feasting, and Jagar performances take place. Nanda Devi temple at Wan is considered extremely sacred.

The Sacred Offering at Homkund
At Homkund, the priests perform the final sacred rituals. Offerings including flowers, fruits, and sacred items are immersed in the glacial lake. The Chausingha ram is then ceremonially sent forward alone — symbolizing Goddess Nanda's final departure to Kailash. This moment is deeply emotional for all participants. Many pilgrims weep openly.

Traditional Foods of the Yatra
Pilgrims sustain themselves on simple mountain food during the journey: mandua ki roti (bread made from finger millet), jhangora ki kheer (barnyard millet pudding), gahat dal (horse gram lentil soup), and locally foraged greens. These foods are nutritious, lightweight, and appropriate for high-altitude conditions.

Music and Instruments
The Yatra procession is accompanied by traditional Uttarakhand music. Key instruments include the dhol (double-headed drum), damau (kettledrum), turhi (long brass horn), and mashakbeen (bagpipe-like wind instrument). The music creates a trance-like atmosphere during ritual performances.

Dress and Attire
Pilgrims traditionally wear simple white or saffron clothing. Women from the mountain communities wear traditional Garhwali and Kumaoni dress including distinctive jewelry and ornate headdresses. The priests wear elaborate ceremonial dress during rituals.

Community and Social Significance
The Yatra transcends religious boundaries and is a major social event uniting the mountain communities. It strengthens bonds between villages, revives traditional knowledge systems, and provides a platform for oral traditions, folk art, and cultural expression that might otherwise be lost.
